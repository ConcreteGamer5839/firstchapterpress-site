A Bitter Blend

The afternoon Emma Calloway got the keys to the tea shop, and the promise Irene Vance didn't keep.

Claire Willowbrook · about a fifteen-minute read

One · Forty-One Boxes of Tea

The moving van left at eleven, and by noon Emma Calloway was sitting on the floor of a shuttered tea shop with forty-one boxes and no idea how to open a business.

The boxes were the problem. She had counted them twice on the drive up from Seattle, because counting was a thing her hands did when the rest of her was panicking, and both times she had come up with forty-one. Thirty-eight were tea. Two were her clothes. One was books, mostly law, which she had told herself she was keeping for the shelf space.

The shop smelled like dust and old cedar and, underneath that, faintly and impossibly, like bergamot. Someone had sold a great deal of Earl Grey in this room for a very long time.

"You're sitting on the floor."

Irene Vance stood in the doorway with a ring of keys in one hand and a paper sack in the other, and she said it the way a person says your shoe is untied — an observation, freely offered, no judgment attached and none withheld either.

"I am," Emma agreed.

"There's a stool."

"There's a stool," Emma said, "and if I sit on the stool I'll have to make a decision about the boxes, and I'm not ready."

Irene made a sound that might have been a laugh. She was somewhere past seventy, narrow and upright, in a green raincoat that had been expensive about fifteen years ago and had been looked after so carefully since that nobody would notice. She crossed the room without hurrying, set the paper sack on the counter, and looked around at the shop she had owned for thirty-one years as though checking it for damage.

"It's smaller than you remember," Emma said.

"It's exactly the size I remember." Irene set the keys down beside the sack. "You're the one who's changed size. You came up in April and you were a lawyer. Now you're a person with forty boxes."

"Forty-one."

"Worse."

Emma got up off the floor, because the alternative was letting a woman of seventy-something stand while she sat, and she'd been raised by a mother who would have felt that through the floorboards from four hundred miles away. Her knees had opinions about the last three hours in the van. She ignored them.

The shop was the ground floor of a blue Victorian on Harbor Street, weathered down to something closer to grey on the west side where the weather came in. There was a bay window that would be wonderful with the right seating and was currently full of a dead fern. There was a long counter of scarred fir. There were shelves — good shelves, deep and solid, built by someone who'd expected them to be used — and behind the counter a doorway to a back room, and behind that a storage room Emma had seen exactly once and had already privately decided to be frightened of.

"Well," Irene said. "Keys."

She slid the ring across the counter. There were four keys on it, and a small brass tag worn smooth of whatever it had once said.

Emma didn't pick them up.

"Can I ask you something," she said, "and will you tell me the truth?"

"Those are two questions, and the answer to the first is yes."

"Why did you sell it to me?"

Irene looked at her and said nothing. That was the thing Emma noticed, and she noticed it the way she'd spent four years noticing things — not because she was clever but because she had been trained, expensively and thoroughly, to watch a person's face at the exact moment a question landed. Most people's faces did something. Irene's declined.

"You offered," Irene said. "That's generally how it works."

"I offered eleven thousand dollars less than the property is worth, in a town where a harbor-facing storefront doesn't come up twice in a decade, and you said yes in a phone call that lasted four minutes." Emma heard her own voice doing the thing it did, the flat careful cadence of cross-examination, and she made herself stop and start again, softer. "I'm not trying to talk you out of it. The money's cleared. It's mine. I just — I spent four years watching people accept bad deals quickly. Not one of them did it to be kind."

The fog had started to come in. It did that here, she'd been told, most afternoons around four, sliding up off the Pacific and down Harbor Street until the shops on the water side turned into suggestions of themselves. It was three-fifteen. Apparently the fog was early too.

Irene picked up the paper sack, unrolled the top, and took out two cups. Not paper cups. Actual cups, white porcelain, one with a hairline crack running up from the base like a river on a map.

"Sit down," she said. "I'll make you something and you'll drink it, and then I'll answer your question, and then I'm going to walk home before this weather gets serious."

"There's no water on."

"There's a kettle in the back and a tap that works and a burner I paid to have inspected in June." Irene was already moving. "You bought a tea shop, Emma. Learn where things are."

•••

The tea was a Darjeeling, second flush, and it was the best thing Emma had tasted in a year.

She said so. Irene received the compliment the way she'd received the earlier observation about the stool — as information, correctly filed.

"There's four pounds of it in the cabinet under the register," she said. "Consider it a housewarming. Mabel Prentiss will smell it on you within a week and she'll want it, and you'll want to say no because you'll be saving it, and I'm telling you now: give it to her. Mabel's been coming in on Tuesdays and Thursdays since 1998. If you keep her, half this town follows her in the door. If you lose her, you'll spend two years wondering why nobody comes."

"Mabel Prentiss," Emma repeated.

"Write it down."

Emma wrote it down, on the back of a receipt, because her notebooks were in a box and she had no idea which one.

Irene watched her do it. Then she set her own cup on the counter, and folded her hands, and said, "I'm selling because I'm tired, and because I'm going to be more tired next year than I am this year, and because there is nobody in Harrow's Cove I would hand this room to who would still be standing in five years."

"That's a nice answer."

"It's a true answer."

"It's a true answer that doesn't have eleven thousand dollars in it."

Irene looked at her then, properly, for what felt like a long moment. Outside, the fog took the Klamath Street corner and folded it away.

"You're going to be a nuisance," Irene said. "Aren't you."

"I've been told."

"By whom?"

"A partner at a firm. And an ex-boyfriend. And, once, memorably, a judge."

"Mm." Irene picked up her cup again, and for a second something did move in her face — not much, and not for long, but Emma had been waiting for it and she caught the edge of it going past. "Ask me again in a fortnight," Irene said. "When you've met a few people and you've got some sense of the shape of the place. You'll ask a better version of the question and I'll give you a better version of the answer. That's a promise, and I keep them, which you'll find is not a universal habit around here."

"A fortnight."

"The eleventh. I'll come by. You'll have the shelves half full and you'll be at the stage where you hate it, and I'll tell you the rest, and you'll feel considerably better about the eleven thousand dollars." She set down the cracked cup. "Don't wash that one. It leaks."

She was at the door before Emma found the thing she wanted to say.

"Irene."

The old woman turned, one hand on the frame, the fog behind her going the color of a nickel.

"Thank you," Emma said. "For the shop. And the Darjeeling. And for not asking me why I left."

Irene Vance smiled — the first real one of the afternoon, quick and surprisingly warm, and it took about fifteen years off her.

"Everyone leaves something," she said. "Only the interesting ones move forty-one boxes to do it."

Then she went out into the weather, and the bell over the door — a real bell, brass, older than Emma — rang once behind her.

•••

Emma worked until eight. She got the dead fern out of the bay window and four of the tea boxes onto the shelves, in an order she would completely reverse within a week, and at some point she stopped noticing the dust because she had become part of it.

She thought about the eleven thousand dollars twice more and then put it away.

That was the part she would come back to, afterwards, over and over, in the long grey fortnight that followed: that she had known something was wrong, and had asked exactly one good question, and had then been talked pleasantly out of asking a second one by a woman she'd liked immediately.

Because Irene Vance did not come by on the eleventh.

On the morning of the eleventh, Irene Vance was found at the bottom of the harbor stairs, on the wet stone below the tide line, with her expensive old raincoat soaked through and one shoe missing.

And the whole of Harrow's Cove said the same thing, kindly and immediately and with no ill will at all:

Those stairs. In this weather. It was only ever a matter of time.

Emma Calloway had spent four years watching people accept a tidy story quickly. She put down the box she was holding and kept her own counsel.

For now.
